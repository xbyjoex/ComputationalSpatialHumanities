\begin{abstract}
Leipzig publishes 398 open datasets and satisfies every requirement open-data
policy imposes. We ingested all of them into a single spatially and temporally
joinable platform and measured what that took. Over nine weeks in which the
portal itself did not change, datasets carrying usable data rose from 152 to
350 and statistical records from 5{,}925 to 377{,}083; the entire difference is
interpretive labour, delivered as five hand-maintained registries and several
hundred curated indicator definitions. We report a taxonomy of the frictions
responsible, including two that fail silently: 56 of 300 spatial metric names
occur in more than one dataset with different base populations, and files mix
administrative levels without marking them, which misplaced 13{,}862 rows in our
own ingestion. A case study over Leipzig's 63 Ortsteile finds electoral turnout
falling sharply with single-parent share and unemployment, and shows that
re-aggregating the identical votes into 10 districts reverses the sign of a
party-level correlation and turns a null result into a significant one. We argue
that publication is not transparency: the interpretive layer between them has a
cost, and whoever can pay it decides what is transparent in practice.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The city of Leipzig publishes 398 datasets from 37 municipal offices on an open
data portal. By the standard any open-data policy applies, the city is
transparent: the data are online, machine-readable, documented, and free.

We tried to use all of them. This paper reports what stood between the published
files and a question anyone could answer with them, and it argues that the
distance is not an implementation detail but the substance of the transparency
claim. Publication and usability are separate achievements, purchased
separately. Only the first is what open-data policy measures, and only the second
is what a citizen needs.

The platform we built to establish this is called \emph{auerbachs-auge.tech},
after Auerbach's Cellar --- the Leipzig tavern where Goethe set a scene of
\emph{Faust}, in which Mephistopheles bores a wooden table and pours wine that
seems abundant until it is touched. It ingests both of Leipzig's data sources
into a single spatially and temporally joinable model, serves them as vector
tiles and time series, and computes correlations between any two indicators that
share a spatial unit and a year.

Our contributions are four.

\begin{itemize}
\item A production system that brings an entire municipal open data catalogue
--- 398 datasets from two sources with incompatible paradigms --- into one model
in which any two indicators can be compared, running on a single 4\,GB server.
\item An empirical taxonomy of the frictions separating published from usable
data, each quantified against the full catalogue, including two classes that
produce silently wrong results rather than visible failures: metric names that
are not unique, and administrative levels mixed within a file without being
marked.
\item A worked spatial case study over Leipzig's 63 Ortsteile, reported together
with the methodological guardrails it requires --- and with a demonstration, on
the city's own published election results, that re-aggregating the identical
data into 10 districts reverses the sign of a party-level correlation and turns
a null result into a significant one.
\item An argument, evidenced by our own constraints rather than asserted, that
the interpretive layer between publication and use has a cost, that the cost is
unevenly borne, and that whoever bears it decides what is transparent in
practice.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper follows that order: Section~\ref{sec:background} situates the argument,
Section~\ref{sec:frictions} measures the frictions, Section~\ref{sec:system} describes the system built against
them, Section~\ref{sec:results} reports coverage and the case study, and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} takes up what the whole exercise implies for the
civic promise of open data.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

Open government data rests on a civic premise: publish the administration's
numbers in machine-readable form and citizens gain the standing to check what
they are told. European and German policy has largely settled the supply side of
that premise, and municipal portals are now routine.

The critical literature has attacked the inference from supply to competence.
Gurstein asks whether open data empowers anyone beyond those already equipped to
use it, and argues that publication without effective use redistributes advantage
rather than knowledge \cite{gurstein2011opendata}. Kitchin, Lauriault and McArdle
show that urban indicator systems and dashboards are not neutral windows but
instruments that shape the governance reading them \cite{kitchin2015knowing}.
Mattern traces the dashboard's descent from mission control and its recurring
promise of mastery through display \cite{mattern2015missioncontrol}. Against the
same tendency, Drucker argues that humanistic visualisation must treat its inputs
as \emph{capta} --- taken and constructed rather than given
\cite{drucker2011humanities}.

Our methodological caveats come from spatial analysis. Tobler's observation that
near things are more related than distant things \cite{tobler1970computer} is why
spatial correlation is worth computing at all. Openshaw's modifiable areal unit
problem is why the result depends on the zoning chosen: aggregating the same
observations into different areal units changes the coefficients, in both scale
and shape \cite{openshaw1984maup}. Robinson's demonstration that ecological
correlations do not transfer to individuals \cite{robinson1950ecological} bounds
what any of it may be said to mean.

What is missing is the layer between. Leipzig's portal lists fifteen
applications built on its data; each serves a single dataset or theme, and none
joins across the catalogue. The critical literature describes what dashboards do
to their readers, and the methodological literature describes how aggregation
misleads, but neither measures what it costs to make a municipal catalogue
comparable in the first place. That measurement is what we report.

\section{The Data Landscape and Its Frictions}
\label{sec:frictions}

By the ordinary standards of open government data, Leipzig's portal is a good
one. It runs on CKAN, exposes DCAT metadata, and publishes 398 datasets from 37
municipal offices across 13 thematic groups. Resources arrive in machine-readable
formats, some are refreshed daily, the catalogue reaches from the tree cadastre
to election results, and the listed contacts answer. Nothing that follows is a
complaint about a negligent administration. Every friction we describe is
compatible with every open-data requirement we are aware of, and that is
precisely the point: compliance is measured at the moment of publication, not at
the moment of use.

We drew from two sources with incompatible paradigms. The CKAN catalogue at
\texttt{opendata.leipzig.de} offers file resources whose format is whatever the
publishing office exported. The statistical office at
\texttt{statistik.leipzig.de} serves a JSON API in a wide-by-year layout, with
years as columns and indicator codes as rows. Neither is wrong. They simply
cannot be read by the same code.

Table~\ref{tab:frictions} lists the eight classes of friction we encountered
while bringing all 398 datasets into one queryable model, each with the extent we
measured. Two deserve emphasis because they are the ones that produced silent
errors rather than loud ones.

\emph{Metric names are not unique.} A metric is identified in the published data
by its name alone, but 56 of the 300 Ortsteil-level metric names we loaded occur
in more than one dataset. The name \texttt{Ausländer} (``foreign nationals'')
appears in six, denominated once against residents, once against registered
residents, once against employees, once against the unemployed, once against
people with a migration background, and once against welfare recipients. Six
quantities, six base populations, one label. Nothing in the published metadata
distinguishes them. The unit of identity is the pair (dataset, metric), and a
consumer has to infer that.

\emph{Administrative levels are mixed without being marked.} A single file
routinely contains rows for Ortsteile, for the Stadtbezirke that contain them,
and for the city as a whole, with no column stating which level a row belongs to.
The level has to be inferred from the area name, and the names do not support the
inference: the Stadtbezirke \emph{Mitte}, \emph{Südost} and \emph{Nordwest} are
each a substring of an unrelated Ortsteil name --- \emph{Grünau-Mitte},
\emph{Zentrum-Südost}, \emph{Zentrum-Nordwest}. Any name-similarity heuristic
conflates them, and the affected rows then carry the population of a district
under the code of a neighbourhood. In our own ingestion this misplaced 13{,}862
rows across 42 datasets; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} returns to what it took to
notice.

The same file structure makes even the city's population ambiguous. Summed over
the 63 Ortsteile, Leipzig had 632{,}560 inhabitants in 2024; summed over the 10
Stadtbezirke, the same 632{,}560; the file's own city row reads 632{,}562. Two
residents are carried without an Ortsteil assignment and appear only in the
total. Even the population of a city depends on which row you read.

\section{System Design}
\label{sec:system}

Section~\ref{sec:frictions} fixes the requirement. If 398 datasets are to be compared rather than
merely downloaded, they must share one spatial axis, one temporal axis, and one
semantic registry. Everything in \emph{auerbachs-auge.tech} exists to supply one
of those three, and Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the seven services that
carry a dataset from the portal to a map in a browser.

\subsection{Pipeline}
Ingestion runs in four stages. Typed extractors fetch each resource over HTTP
with retry and back-off, and record every attempt in a \texttt{raw\_ingest} audit
schema, so a dataset that silently stops updating is visible as a run that
fetched nothing rather than as a chart that stopped moving. A \texttt{staging}
schema holds intermediate normalisation, including the melt that turns
wide-by-year statistical layouts into long records. Loaders then upsert into a
normalised \texttt{core} schema, and materialised views in a \texttt{mart} schema
are refreshed concurrently after each run. A nightly pass covers all datasets; a
five-minute loop covers the three that are genuinely live.

\subsection{The registries are the contribution}
The part of this system that took the most work is not the pipeline. It is five
hand-maintained JSON registries that supply the meaning the portal does not ship.
\texttt{dataset\_contracts.json} names, for each of the 398 datasets, which
resource to fetch, in which format, on which schedule, and whether it carries
geometry. \texttt{dataset\_families.json} merges annual variants into one logical
dataset with a year dimension, and carries per-dataset hints where a loader
heuristic guesses wrong. \texttt{dataset\_categories.json} reconciles the
portal's CKAN groups with the statistical office's numeric category scheme.
\texttt{election\_definitions.json} maps result columns onto parties for each
election, verified against the named vote shares the statistical API reports
separately. \texttt{indicator\_catalog.json} is a curated register of indicator
names, units and topics; the user interface never exposes a raw metric name,
because Section~\ref{sec:frictions} is what raw metric names look like.

We report this plainly because it is the paper's central empirical claim about
cost. These five files are not configuration in the ordinary sense. They are a
few thousand lines of human judgement about what published numbers mean, written
by two people over one semester, and they are the difference between 398
downloadable files and 398 comparable ones.

\subsection{Resolving space}
The single most consequential decision in the system is how a spatial reference
becomes a key. Raw data name places inconsistently: \texttt{Zentrum-Südost},
\texttt{Zentrum-Suedost}, \texttt{02}, \texttt{Schönef.-Abtnaundorf}. A resolver
maps each raw key through an alias table onto a canonical code in a boundary
table holding Leipzig's 63 Ortsteile, 10 Stadtbezirke and 819 electoral
districts, trying exact alias, exact code and embedded code before falling back
to fuzzy name matching. Every statistic that carries a spatial reference is
joined on that canonical code, which is what makes a correlation between two
unrelated datasets possible at all. It is also, as Section~\ref{sec:frictions} noted and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} takes up, where our own worst error occurred.

\subsection{Serving}
Geometry is served as vector tiles generated in the database with PostGIS
\texttt{ST\_AsMVT} and cached per tile in Redis, invalidated by a version bump
after each nightly run; 489{,}011 features across 35 datasets remain
interactive on modest hardware because the client never receives the underlying
geometry. Statistics are served as JSON by an asynchronous API. The client is a
React single-page application drawing MapLibre GL vector tiles.

\subsection{Operating envelope}
The whole system --- database, cache, API, ETL scheduler, static frontend,
reverse proxy and monitoring --- runs in Docker Compose on a single virtual
server with 4\,GB of RAM, deployed from a Git push. Access is authenticated. We
return to both of these facts in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, because they are
not incidental to the argument: they are the argument.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Coverage, and what the coverage measures}
Table~\ref{tab:coverage} reports the state of the ingested corpus at two points
nine weeks apart. Datasets carrying usable data rose from 152 to 350 of 398;
statistical records from 5{,}925 to 377{,}083; election results from none to
79{,}553.

The portal did not change between those two measurements. No office published
anything new, no format improved, no schema was fixed. The entire difference is
interpretive labour: melt rules for wide-by-year layouts, alias entries for place
names, column-to-party mappings, curated indicator definitions. That is what
makes the table a measurement rather than a progress report. \emph{The distance
between published and usable data is the size of that delta.}

Two rows fall rather than rise, and both are corrections. Geometry features drop
from 4{,}175{,}146 to 489{,}011 and traffic restrictions from 30{,}999 to 1{,}421
because several sources issue fresh feature identifiers on every fetch, so each
nightly run re-inserted a full snapshot until the loaders learned to sweep what a
run did not touch. The smaller numbers are the correct ones.

What survives as an analytical surface is narrower than 350 datasets suggests:
341{,}945 records across 67 datasets and 300 metrics carry a resolved Ortsteil
code, and five elections are stored at electoral-district level, four of them
also aggregated to Ortsteile.

\subsection{A worked case: participation and social structure}
We pair the 2024 European election with three indicators built from summable
counts and renormalised at each spatial level: registered unemployed per
resident, foreign nationals per resident, and single-parent families per family
with children. Election and indicators share a year and a spatial unit; the
platform refuses pairings that do not.

The strongest relationships in the grid are not about party choice but about
participation. Turnout falls as the single-parent share rises ($r=-0.765$,
$R^2=0.585$, $n=63$; Fig.~\ref{fig:scatter}) and as unemployment rises
($r=-0.703$). Turnout itself ranges from 51\,\% in Grünau-Nord to 83\,\% in
Baalsdorf (Fig.~\ref{fig:choropleth}) --- a 32-point spread inside one city in one
election. Among parties, the clearest signal is a mirror image: CDU vote share
falls with unemployment ($r=-0.627$) where the Left party's rises ($r=+0.598$).

The pairing a citizen would most likely reach for is the one that fails. AfD
share against unemployment yields $r=-0.114$ at $p=0.37$: no relationship at all
across Leipzig's 63 Ortsteile.

\subsection{The same data, zoned differently}
\label{sec:maup}
Leipzig's 63 Ortsteile nest into 10 Stadtbezirke, and the 2024 election is
reported officially at both levels. Recomputing the identical pairing at the
coarser level moves that null result to $r=+0.646$ at $p=0.04$ ---
significant, positive, and opposite in sign (Fig.~\ref{fig:maup}). Ten of 21
pairings reach $p<0.05$ over Ortsteile and seven over Stadtbezirke, but not the
same seven; several reverse sign.

This is Openshaw's scale effect \cite{openshaw1984maup}, and it is the reason
this paper is named after Mephistopheles' warning to the student that all theory
is grey \cite{goethe1808faust}. Neither coefficient is the true one. At $n=10$ a
single district can carry the fit, we apply no correction for testing 21
pairings, and no aggregate correlation licenses any claim about individual voters
\cite{robinson1950ecological}. The finding is not that the relationship reverses.
It is that a competent analyst, working honestly from published municipal data,
can obtain either result and has nothing in the data to tell them which zoning to
choose.

\section{Discussion: Transparency and Its Infrastructure}
\label{sec:discussion}

Open data is defended on the ground that it makes citizens competent to judge:
given the numbers, people can form views from evidence rather than from
assertion. We hold that position. This paper is an argument about what it costs
to make it true.

\subsection{Publication is not the expensive part}
Leipzig discharged its obligation the moment the 398 datasets went online. What
remained was the work in Section~\ref{sec:system}: five hand-maintained registries, roughly 300
metric definitions, an alias table for place names, and a resolver deciding what
a row's spatial reference means. None of that is discoverable from the data. All
of it is required before two datasets can be compared. Gurstein's objection to
open data --- that it empowers those already equipped
\cite{gurstein2011opendata} --- names exactly this gap, and Table~\ref{tab:frictions} is a
measurement of it. A citizen with a spreadsheet and an afternoon does not clear
it. Two computer science students with a semester barely did.

\subsection{What it took to notice our own error}
Section~\ref{sec:frictions} described a source property: administrative levels mixed in one file
with no column marking them. We were the consumer that guessed, and we guessed
wrong. Our resolver's fuzzy fallback matched the Stadtbezirk \emph{Mitte} onto
the Ortsteil \emph{Grünau-Mitte}, and two of its siblings likewise, putting
13{,}862 rows across 42 datasets under the wrong spatial code and inflating
Leipzig's 2024 population to 805{,}218.

Nothing about the resulting data looked wrong. Every map rendered, every chart
had a plausible shape, every correlation was finite and interpretable. We found
the error only because a total we computed disagreed with a total we already
knew. That is worth stating precisely, because it defines the standard: data
carrying the wrong meaning is not visibly different from data carrying the right
one, and no amount of looking at a dashboard reveals the difference. Drucker's
point that data are \emph{capta} --- taken, not given, constructed as an
interpretation \cite{drucker2011humanities} --- is not a philosophical caveat
here. It is an operational one. Someone constructed those spatial codes, that
someone was us, and we were wrong.

\subsection{Transparency has an infrastructure cost}
Our platform requires a login, and its metric list is curated rather than
complete. Both cut against the openness we are arguing for, and neither is
ideological. The system runs on a single 4\,GB server: we have no budget for
rate-limiting abuse or absorbing a crawl, and an uncurated metric list surfaces
\texttt{Unnamed:~10} beside real indicators, which misleads more than it informs.
They are capacity decisions.

That is the finding, not an apology. Two people who wanted maximal openness could
not afford it. The interpretive layer that turns published data into usable data
costs money, skill and continuous maintenance, and whoever can pay for it decides
what is in practice transparent. Publishing more datasets does not distribute
that capacity; it presumes it. If open data is to serve the competence it
promises, the interpretive layer belongs to public infrastructure rather than to
volunteers, and cities should be measured on whether their data can be used
rather than on how much of it exists.

Four changes would do more for usability than any additional dataset: a stable
spatial key shared across datasets; long-format publication instead of
wide-by-year; machine-readable indicator definitions naming each metric's
denominator; and one dataset per phenomenon with a year dimension instead of one
per year.

\subsection{What a dashboard cannot do}
Mattern's history of the urban dashboard warns against the genre's implicit
promise of mastery through display \cite{mattern2015missioncontrol}, and Kitchin
et al. show how indicator systems shape the governance that reads them
\cite{kitchin2015knowing}. Our own results argue the same way from the inside.
The platform will happily show a user the correlation in
Section~\ref{sec:maup} at either spatial scale, and it cannot tell them which to
believe --- because the data cannot. A dashboard lowers one barrier: it makes
comparison possible. It does not make comparison sound. Evidence-based
citizenship needs both, and the second is a harder engineering problem than the
first.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}

Our platform is authenticated, so it does not itself demonstrate the public
access it argues for. Forty-eight of the 398 datasets still carry no data, mostly
statistical layouts our melt rules do not yet recognise. The case study covers
one election in one city: $n=63$ at the finer scale and $n=10$ at the coarser,
with no correction for multiple comparisons and no causal claim of any kind. The
before-and-after coverage figures come from two audits of our own database rather
than from an independently reproducible baseline. Accessibility of the interface
is unaddressed. And the whole system depends on the portal continuing to publish
in the shapes we learned.

The obvious extension is a second city, which would separate what is true of
municipal open data from what is true of Leipzig. Beyond that, the registries in
Section~\ref{sec:system} are the artefact most worth sharing: if a comparable catalogue can be
described in the same form, the interpretive layer becomes portable rather than
rebuilt each time.

\section{Conclusion}

Leipzig publishes 398 datasets and satisfies every open-data requirement we know
of. Making them comparable took five hand-maintained registries, several hundred
curated indicator definitions, and a spatial resolver whose failure we did not
detect until a computed population disagreed with a known one. Between those two
states, the portal did not change; the labour did. That labour is the real
subject of the transparency claim. Our case study finds turnout in Leipzig's
Ortsteile falling sharply with the single-parent share and with unemployment,
and finds the same votes yielding opposite conclusions about party support
depending only on how the city is divided. Both findings are available to any
citizen who downloads the data. Neither is available to a citizen who only
downloads it.
